How it works

Star trackers find their direction from star patterns. This solver does the same with craters: it finds crater patterns in the image, looks them up in a catalog of the Moon, and computes where the camera must have been.

Overview

The input is one image. Nothing else is necessary: no time, no approximate position, no camera data. The output is the camera latitude, longitude and altitude, the direction the camera pointed, and the latitude and longitude of every pixel.

  1. Find cratersneural network
  2. Hash patterns4 craters → 8 numbers
  3. Look up8.6 M patterns, k-d tree
  4. Verifylog-odds against chance
  5. Fit camerarobust least squares

The method follows astrometry.net, the blind star-field solver, with craters in place of stars and crater diameter in place of brightness. Two things are new compared with stars: craters have a size, which the hash uses; and the Moon is a nearby sphere seen in perspective, not a celestial sphere at infinity, so the last step fits a full 3-D camera.

1. Frames and camera model

Positions are in the Moon-fixed (mean Earth / polar axis) frame: origin at the Moon's centre, \(z\) to the north pole, \(x\) to latitude 0°, longitude 0°. The mean radius is \(R = 1737.4\) km. A point at latitude \(\varphi\), longitude \(\lambda\) and height \(h\) is

$$\mathbf{X} = (R + h)\,\big(\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\; \cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\; \sin\varphi\big).$$

The camera is a pinhole with centre \(\mathbf{C}\), rotation \(R_{wc}\) (world to camera; camera axes \(x\) right, \(y\) down, \(z\) along the boresight), focal length \(f\) in pixels and principal point \((c_x, c_y)\):

$$\mathbf{X}_c = R_{wc}(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{C}), \qquad \pi(\mathbf{X}) = \Big(c_x + f\,\tfrac{X_c}{Z_c},\;\; c_y + f\,\tfrac{Y_c}{Z_c}\Big).$$

The altitude is \(|\mathbf{C}| - R\). The inverse, pixel to latitude/longitude, intersects the ray \(\mathbf{C} + t\,R_{wc}^{\top}\big((u-c_x)/f,\,(v-c_y)/f,\,1\big)\) with the LOLA terrain (fixed-point iteration on the sphere of radius \(R + h(\varphi, \lambda)\)), not with the mean sphere: in an oblique view the mean sphere is off by \(h\tan\theta\) at viewing angle \(\theta\).

2. Crater detection (CraterNet)

Network. A U-Net with residual blocks: encoder widths 24, 32, 64, 128, 192, 256 channels, 6.2 million parameters, output at stride 2 (half the input resolution). Each output cell gives

  • \(\hat Y\): probability that a crater centre is in the cell (sigmoid of a logit),
  • \((\delta_x, \delta_y)\): sub-cell offset of that centre,
  • \(\ln \hat r\): logarithm of the radius in input pixels.

Targets and loss (CenterNet style). For a crater at cell position \(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\) with radius \(r\), the heatmap target is a Gaussian of width \(\sigma = \max(0.6,\, r/8)\) cells, \(Y_{\mathbf{q}} = \max_k \exp\!\big(-\lVert\mathbf{q} - \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_k\rVert^2 / 2\sigma_k^2\big)\). The heatmap loss is the penalty-reduced focal loss with \(\alpha = 2\), \(\beta = 4\):

$$L_{\text{hm}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}} \begin{cases}(1-\hat Y_{\mathbf{q}})^{\alpha}\,\ln \hat Y_{\mathbf{q}} & Y_{\mathbf{q}} = 1\\[2pt] (1-Y_{\mathbf{q}})^{\beta}\,\hat Y_{\mathbf{q}}^{\alpha}\,\ln(1-\hat Y_{\mathbf{q}}) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

plus L1 losses on the offset and on \(\ln r\) at the true centres. Craters outside the band of radii that a pyramid level is trained for are masked ("ignore" regions): they are not targets, and detections on them are not punished.

Training data. Generated on the GPU, with labels from the crater catalog: (a) the LOLA elevation model rendered with a random sun (elevation drawn from 1.5°–89°), cast shadows, Lunar-Lambert reflectance with the McEwen phase function \(L(g) = 1 - 0.019g + 2.42\cdot10^{-4}g^2 - 1.46\cdot10^{-6}g^3\), and Clementine 750 nm albedo; (b) crops of the LRO WAC mosaic; (c) noise, blur, contrast and tilt. Longitudes 330°–360° are held out for testing.

Inference. An image pyramid (factor 2 per level) covers all sizes: level 0 keeps radii 3–36 px, the other levels 14–36 px, the last level up to 48 px. Cells with \(\hat Y \ge 0.3\) that are local maxima give detections; non-maximum suppression removes duplicates closer than \(0.4\,r\) with \(|\Delta \ln r| < 0.35\). The solver uses detections with \(\hat Y \ge 0.35\).

3. The crater-pattern hash

A quad is four craters A, B, C, D in which C and D lie inside the circle that has AB as its diameter; so A and B are the pair farthest apart. With image (or map) positions \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}\) and \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\), the positions of C and D in the frame where A is \((0, 0)\) and B is \((1, 0)\) are

$$x_C = \frac{(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{e}}{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert^2}, \qquad y_C = \frac{\mathbf{e}\times(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{a})}{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert^2},$$

and the same for D (\(\times\) is the 2-D cross product). These four numbers do not change under translation, rotation and scaling of the image.

Canonical order. The same four craters can be labelled in several ways. The labelling is fixed by two rules: if \(x_C + x_D > 1\), swap A and B (which maps \(x \to 1-x,\ y \to -y\)); then if \(x_C > x_D\), swap C and D.

Sizes. Unlike stars, craters have a radius. With \(m\) the mean of \(\ln r\) over the four craters, the code adds the relative radii and the size of the craters relative to their spacing:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{k} = \Big(&x_C,\; y_C,\; x_D,\; y_D,\\ &w_R(\ln r_A - m),\; w_R(\ln r_B - m),\; w_R(\ln r_C - m),\; w_S\big(m - \ln\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert\big)\Big) \in \mathbb{R}^8.\end{aligned}$$

The \(D\) radius term is left out because the four terms sum to zero. The weights whiten the noise: the position terms have a typical error of about 0.02, and \(\ln r\) about 0.08, so \(w_R = 0.30\) makes the radius terms comparable; \(w_S = 0.15\) is smaller because it also absorbs any bias of the detector's radii. The weights and the match tolerance came from a sweep of recall against false hits.

Near a boundary. If \(|x_C + x_D - 1| < 2\tau\) or \(|x_C - x_D| < 2\tau\), noise may have flipped the labelling relative to the index, so the solver also queries the swapped variants.

4. The index

The catalog is the Robbins (2019) database: 1,296,796 craters of 1–2 km and larger. The index is built once.

  • Size bands. Band \(l = 0, \dots, 14\) holds quads with \(\lVert AB\rVert \in [S_0\,2^{l/2},\; S_0\,2^{(l+1)/2})\), \(S_0 = 8\) km: from 8 km to about 1,400 km.
  • Even coverage. In each band, \(n = 4\pi R^2 / s^2\) sample points with spacing \(s = S_0 2^{l/2} / 1.5\) are placed on a Fibonacci lattice: point \(k\) has \(z_k = 1 - \frac{2k+1}{n}\) and longitude \(k \cdot \pi(3 - \sqrt5)\) (the golden angle).
  • Brightest stars per cell. At each point, the \(M = 9\) largest nearby craters of a size suited to the band form up to \(Q = 6\) quads, in gnomonic (tangent-plane) coordinates around the point: $$x = \frac{\cos\varphi\,\sin(\lambda-\lambda_0)}{\cos c},\quad y = \frac{\cos\varphi_0\sin\varphi - \sin\varphi_0\cos\varphi\cos(\lambda-\lambda_0)}{\cos c},$$ \(\cos c = \sin\varphi_0\sin\varphi + \cos\varphi_0\cos\varphi\cos(\lambda-\lambda_0)\).
  • Result. 8.6 million quads; their codes go into a k-d tree. A query returns every index code within Euclidean distance \(\tau = 0.08\) of the image code.

Row numbers in the index refer to the catalog sorted by diameter. Many diameters are equal, and the default sort of NumPy orders equal values differently on different CPUs, so the order is stored once with the data.

5. Search order

Query quads come cheapest-first from three sources, interleaved:

  1. the \(N\) largest detections, \(N = 8, 11, 14, 18, 23, 30, 40\) (like the brightest stars);
  2. a local rule that mirrors the index: at each pixel scale, a grid of cells and the few largest suitable detections in each cell;
  3. "oblique" guesses: before hashing, undo an assumed foreshortening (tilt 20°–55° toward six azimuths), for strongly tilted views.

For each query, hits are tested in order of code distance (true matches sit near distance 0, chance hits fill the tolerance ball uniformly and pile up near its edge). Each image is tried as taken and mirrored. A vectorised pre-filter rejects most hits cheaply: it predicts where neighbouring catalog craters should appear and requires at least 3 of 12 to be found, using k-d trees binned by \(\ln\) diameter.

6. Verification: log-odds

A hit is a hypothesis: "these four image craters are those four catalog craters". It gives a map from the image to the gnomonic plane around the quad. The map grows with the support: a similarity at first, then an affine map, then a homography, over neighbourhoods of 2, 4 and 8 times \(\lVert AB\rVert\), then the whole image. Through the map, every catalog crater predicts a position and radius in the image.

Detection \(i\) (radius \(r_i\) px, implied diameter \(d_i = 2 r_i s\) km at \(s\) km/px) is matched when a predicted crater lies within \(\tau_i = \max(0.5\,r_i,\, 2\text{ px})\) and \(|\ln(r_{\text{cat}}/r_i)| < 0.4\). The two hypotheses are compared per detection:

$$p_{\text{bg},i} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{n_i\,\pi\tau_i^2}{A},\ 10^{-4},\ 0.9\right), \qquad p_{\text{m},i} = 0.6\cdot\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{d_i - d_0}{d_1 - d_0},\ 0.15,\ 1\right),$$

where \(n_i\) is the number of catalog craters in view with a radius similar to \(r_i\), \(A\) the image area, and \((d_0, d_1) = (0.7, 2.5)\) km the catalog's completeness ramp (the probability that a real crater of that size is in the catalog). The evidence is

$$\Lambda = \sum_{i\ \in\ \text{matched}} \ln\frac{p_{\text{m},i}}{p_{\text{bg},i}} \;+\; \sum_{i\ \notin\ \text{matched}} \ln\frac{1 - p_{\text{m},i}}{1 - p_{\text{bg},i}}.$$

Detections below the catalog's completeness carry no evidence, and neither do detections that the camera sees more than 60° from the surface normal: there craters are thin ellipses, the detector misses many of them and gives radii that do not match.

Accept whenMeaning
\(\Lambda \ge 60\)odds above \(e^{60} \approx 10^{26}\) to 1 against chance
\(\Lambda \ge 25\), \(\ge 10\) matches, \(\ge 45\%\) of the 20 largest detections matcheda moderate match that also explains the big craters
\(\Lambda \ge 18\), \(\ge 10\) matches, \(\ge 70\%\) of the 20 largest matchedweak, but covers the dominant craters

These rules came from tests on thousands of simulated views; the solver gives no answer rather than a wrong one. The final \(\Lambda\) is measured after the camera fit, so it is optimistic; that is why the coverage of the largest craters is required for weaker matches.

7. Camera fit and uncertainty

Each matched crater \(i\) gives a 3-D point \(\mathbf{X}_i\): the catalog centre lifted to \(R + h_i\), where \(h_i\) is the mean LOLA height of 12 points on the crater's rim (what the detector sees). The pose is the robust least-squares solution (perspective-n-point)

$$\min_{\mathbf{C},\ \boldsymbol{\omega}\ [,\, f]}\ \sum_i \rho\!\left(\frac{\lVert \pi(\mathbf{X}_i) - \mathbf{u}_i\rVert^2}{(2\sigma_i)^2}\right), \qquad \sigma_i = 0.06\,r_i + 0.5\ \text{px}, \qquad \rho(z) = 2\big(\sqrt{1+z} - 1\big),$$

with the rotation updated through a rotation vector \(\boldsymbol\omega\) (Rodrigues) and \(\rho\) the soft-L1 loss with scale 2: quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones, so wrong matches pull less. Large craters get a larger \(\sigma_i\) because their detected centre is less precise.

Unknown focal length. A camera far away with a narrow lens and a camera close by with a wide lens give nearly the same image: in \(\pi\), \(f\) and the distance \(Z_c\) appear only as \(f/Z_c\). The solver therefore profiles the fit over 10 fixed fields of view from 0.5° to 120° (log-spaced), refines around the best one by factors 0.6–1.6, re-matches all detections through each camera, and then frees \(f\).

Uncertainty. From the Jacobian \(J\) at the optimum, \(\Sigma = (J^\top J)^{-1}\,\hat s^2\) with \(\hat s^2\) the residual variance. The altitude 1-sigma is \(\sigma_{\text{alt}} = \sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{z}}^\top \Sigma_C \hat{\mathbf{z}}}\) (\(\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{C}/|\mathbf{C}|\)), and the horizontal one the trace of \(\Sigma_C\) projected on the tangent plane. Without the focal length, \(\sigma_{\text{alt}}\) is at least half the spread of altitudes over the profile points that fit almost as well.

8. Oblique views: the limb cone

From \(\mathbf{C}\), the limb is the set of rays tangent to the sphere: a cone around the direction \(\hat{\mathbf a}\) to the Moon's centre with half-angle \(\rho\),

$$\sin\rho = \frac{R_{\text{limb}}}{|\mathbf{C}|}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\hat{\mathbf a} = \cos\rho \ \text{ for every limb ray } \hat{\mathbf d},$$

with \(R_{\text{limb}} = R + 1.5\) km (the silhouette is formed by high terrain). Limb pixels are found on the boundary with dark sky; RANSAC fits \((\hat{\mathbf a}, \rho)\) for a few candidate focal lengths. That gives the tilt and the altitude \(|\mathbf{C}| - R\). The image is then re-projected to a virtual camera at the same point looking straight down, a pure rotation, so the homography \(H = K_v R_{vc} K^{-1}\); craters are round again and the normal solver runs on that view. The crater altitude must agree with the limb altitude within 20%.

9. Far views: the disk

A telescope on Earth or a spacecraft thousands of km away sees the whole disk, or part of its edge. The lit limb is a circle in the image with centre \(\mathbf{c}\) and radius \(r\) px, found by RANSAC on the sharp part of the boundary with the sky (the terminator is soft and is ignored). For a camera at distance \(D\) from the Moon's centre with focal length \(f\),

$$\sin\rho = \frac{R}{D}, \qquad r = f\tan\rho \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D = \frac{R}{\sin\big(\arctan(r/f)\big)}.$$

Each pixel inside the circle then maps to exactly one surface point: intersect its ray with the sphere. In camera axes that point has the unit vector \(\mathbf{n}(u, v)\); for a distant camera, \(\mathbf{n} \approx \big(q_x,\, q_y,\, -\sqrt{1 - q_x^2 - q_y^2}\big)\) with \(\mathbf q = (\mathbf u - \mathbf c)/r\). A catalog crater with Moon-frame unit vector \(\mathbf{p}\) appears where \(\mathbf{n} = R_{wc}\mathbf{p}\), so the rotation follows in closed form from the matched pairs (Kabsch / Wahba):

$$H = \sum_i w_i\, \mathbf{p}_i \mathbf{n}_i^{\top} = U S V^{\top}, \qquad R_{wc} = V\,\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1,\, 1,\, \det(V U^{\top})\big)\,U^{\top}.$$

Because this is the exact sphere, a match in one region extends correctly to the whole disk. A short limb arc (a close-up) fixes the circle only to a few percent, so the rotation and the circle are then refined together on the craters and the limb points:

$$\min_{R_{wc},\, \mathbf{c},\, r}\ \sum_i \rho\!\left(\frac{\lVert \pi(R_{wc}\mathbf{p}_i;\, \mathbf c, r) - \mathbf{u}_i\rVert^2}{\sigma_i^2}\right) + \sum_j \rho\!\left(\frac{(\lVert \mathbf{l}_j - \mathbf{c}\rVert - r)^2}{\sigma_{\text{limb}}^2}\right).$$

A close-up near the limb is first re-projected to a straight-down view over its centre, where craters are round, solved there, and mapped back. Distance: with the field of view, \(D\) follows from the formula above. Without it, a far view does not determine \(D\) (between 50,000 and 400,000 km the image changes by less than 0.1%), and the result says so. The direction the camera looked from (the sub-observer point) is always measured.

Our own crater catalog

The hash uses the largest craters the detector finds, like the brightest stars. An index built from a catalog made by other means contains many craters that the detector does not see: in the LRO WAC mosaic it finds only 41% of the 1–2 km and 33% of the 2–5 km Robbins craters (degraded rims, high sun). Patterns built from those craters can never match an image.

So the detector also built a catalog. It read the whole Moon in nine passes: the WAC mosaic at 0.1, 0.4 and 1.6 km/px, and LOLA shaded relief with the sun from four directions (no albedo, other lighting than the mosaic). Detections are clustered across passes (centres within \(0.35\,D\), \(|\Delta\ln D| < 0.35\)); a crater is kept when two passes agree, or one pass is very confident. Craters that match a Robbins crater keep its position and size. The result has 2.56 million craters, 1.27 million of them new (mostly 0.5–1 km), and its quad index (15.3 million quads) uses only craters the detector sees.

The solver tries Robbins first and this catalog second. Robbins is best for large views; the new catalog solves small views: Apollo crops 23 km wide from 3 of 36 to 15 of 36, and 45 km wide from 24 to 32 of 36.

10. Several cameras (rig)

Cameras on one rigid mount, triggered together, with known mounting rotations \(R_{cr,i}\) and offsets \(\mathbf t_i\), share one pose \((\mathbf C, R_{wr})\):

$$R_{wc,i} = R_{cr,i}\,R_{wr}, \qquad \mathbf C_i = \mathbf C + R_{wr}^{\top}\mathbf t_i.$$

One camera is solved blind; it predicts the others, whose craters are then matched (with an offset vote to absorb the first prediction's error), and one robust fit of the 6 pose parameters uses all matches. Views in different directions remove the trade-off between tilt and position of a single narrow camera: in tests the 3-D error fell from about 1.1 km (one camera) to 0.19 km.

11. Dense registration (experimental)

After the crater fit, the image can be matched pixel by pixel to the LROC WAC mosaic rendered on LOLA terrain through the current camera. Square patches are matched by normalised cross-correlation,

$$\text{NCC}(a, b) = \frac{\sum (a - \bar a)(b - \bar b)}{\sqrt{\sum (a-\bar a)^2 \sum (b-\bar b)^2}},$$

each match is a 2-D/3-D correspondence, and the pose is refitted (3–4 rounds, shrinking search). With the same lighting as the map, the error falls from about 800 m to about 10 m. Shadows move with the sun, so for photos with other lighting the matches are biased; a gate accepts the result only when the global correlation is at least 0.9. It is off on the public server.

Limits

  • High sun. Near full Moon, craters show few shadows and the network finds fewer of them.
  • Small footprints. The catalog has craters of about 1 km and larger. An image needs about 15 of them, so views less than about 25 km across rarely solve.
  • Compressed images. Strong JPEG compression creates false craters in flat areas. Use PNG or high-quality JPEG when you can.
  • Distance in far views. Without the field of view, the distance of a telescope image cannot be measured; enter it under Options.
  • Renders and art. Many Moon images online are paintings or 3-D renders with invented craters.

API

The website uses an HTTP API. The public server accepts solves only from this website, after an automatic human check (Cloudflare Turnstile), and limits the number of solves per visitor. To call the API from your own programs, run the server on your computer (python server/api.py, see the README):

curl -F image=@photo.png -F fov_deg=20 http://127.0.0.1:8000/api/solve
